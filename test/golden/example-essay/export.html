Native Title after Mabo

I Introduction

In Mabo v Queensland [No 2], the High Court rejected the proposition that Australia was terra nullius when it was settled, and recognised that the common law could acknowledge the rights of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples to their land.1 Brennan J said that the common law should not be ‘frozen in an age of racial discrimination’.2

Parliament responded with the Native Title Act 1993 (Cth),3 which the Prime Minister described as a response to the High Court’s decision.4

II The Content of Native Title

A Recognition

The Act defines native title by reference to the traditional laws and customs of the people concerned, and their connection with the land or waters.5 The starting point remains Brennan J’s rejection of the earlier doctrine:

Whatever the justification advanced in earlier days for refusing to recognize the rights and interests in land of the indigenous inhabitants of settled colonies, an unjust and discriminatory doctrine of that kind can no longer be accepted. The expectations of the international community accord in this respect with the contemporary values of the Australian people.6

International law has been an influence on that development.7

B Extinguishment

A pastoral lease does not necessarily extinguish native title.8 Native title has been described as a ‘bundle of rights’, some of which may be extinguished while others survive.9 The case law since Mabo has been the subject of extensive commentary.10

III Wider Questions

How a court comes to recognise the laws of another normative system raises the question that legal theory asks of every legal system: what criteria identify its law?11 Encroachments by Commonwealth laws on property rights more generally have been reviewed by the Australian Law Reform Commission.12 The High Court has since held that Aboriginal Australians are not ‘aliens’ within the meaning of the Constitution.13 Whether the reasoning in that decision will develop further remains open.
